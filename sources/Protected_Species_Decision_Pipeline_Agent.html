Bayesian Anomaly Detector — Higher-Than-Expected Interactions

Flags a species-year as anomalous when the current interaction count falls in the extreme upper tail of a Bayesian posterior predictive distribution fit to that species' own historical record (Gamma-Poisson conjugate model, moment-matched to each species' historical mean/P10/P90 from Table 0). Anomaly = higher than expected only; unusually low counts are out of scope for this tool.

0.05 ← species in the top (α×100)% tail of their own history flag as anomalies

Elicit α — Structured Decision Making (Value-Focused Thinking)

Value-focused thinking starts from what the Plan Team actually cares about, not from the statistical knob itself. The two fundamental objectives in tension here are minimizing missed anomalies and minimizing wasted review effort on false alarms — α is just the means objective that trades them off. Rank the objectives, swing-weight them, and ground your prior in what Table 2 actually shows — the implied α is derived, not guessed.

Step 1 — Rank the objectives, then swing-weight
Missed anomaly (top-ranked, anchor)100

A real problem goes unflagged — delayed management response, conservation/legal risk.

False alarm (relative swing)50

A typical year gets escalated — wasted Plan Team review time, "crying wolf" credibility cost.

Swing weighting: whichever objective you rank first is anchored at 100 (its full swing from worst to best outcome). Move the other slider to say how much its swing matters relative to the anchor — 50 means "half as much," 100 means "just as much."

Formula (a transparent value-equalization heuristic, not a full Neyman-Pearson power calculation): α* = (π×r) ÷ ((1−π) + π×r), where r = swing-weight(missed anomaly) ÷ swing-weight(false alarm) and π = prior true-anomaly rate. At r=1 and π=5%, this recovers the conventional α=0.05 default, which is why that's the detector's starting point above. Treat the result as a documented, auditable starting point for Plan Team discussion — not a substitute for a full quantitative risk analysis.

Model: annual count X ~ Poisson(λ), with λ itself uncertain and given a Gamma(α,β) prior fit by method-of-moments to each species' historical mean/P10/P90 (Est.SD ≈ (P90−P10)/2.563, a normal-quantile approximation of historical spread — refine with the true historical variance if per-year raw counts become available). The Gamma-Poisson pairing is conjugate, so P(X ≥ Current) is the exact Negative-Binomial posterior predictive tail probability, computed via the Negative-Binomial–Incomplete-Beta identity rather than approximated.

Oceanic whitetip shark's current count is well below its historical mean — it will always read "Typical" here since this tool only screens for higher-than-expected counts. That species' unusually low count is a distinct anomaly requiring separate review, not this table.

Relative to a rolling-maximum ITS threshold (see the benchmark control on the Dashboard tab), this posterior-predictive approach is directly population-referenced — anchored to each species' fitted λ distribution rather than just its highest historical year — worth weighing against PBR- or demographic-model-anchored thresholds as ITS methodology is refined.

LOTUS observer data in the SAFE report

Observer data doesn't reach the SAFE report directly from the field. It moves through a specific chain, and only the last stage is treated as report-ready:

  • Observers record interactions on tablets during trips, which upload into Pirops (the Pacific Islands Regional Observer Program debriefing database), where automated and staff validations clean and QA the records.
  • Once approved, records move into LOTUS, the backend considered "blessed" — this is the only stage NOAA staff pull from for SAFE report tables; Pirops itself is not queried directly for reporting.
  • A staff analyst runs a set of standing SQL queries against LOTUS — one query per data point — to populate the protected species section of the SAFE report, which is predominantly longline interaction data.
  • Interaction rates are reported as nominal CPUE (catch/interactions per unit effort) rather than raw counts, so a rate change can be distinguished from a simple change in fishing effort.
  • Before disclosure, an aggregation check withholds any stratum observed on fewer than three vessels, to avoid identifying individual vessels.
  • If a discrepancy shows up between LOTUS and Pirops (e.g. a species misidentification later corrected), the record can be pulled back into Pirops, corrected, and pushed forward again — LOTUS is treated as current-best, not immutable.

Today this is a largely manual, once-a-year pull: queries tracked in a spreadsheet, results copied into a Word document, then cross-checked against a second observer-database view for inconsistencies. That manual pull is exactly what Table 0 and the Stage 1 → Stage 2 automation in this pipeline are meant to replace with a standing connection to LOTUS.

A related published tool: PIFSC's Bycatch GUI already exposes recent and historical Hawai'i and American Samoa observed bycatch and bycatch estimates drawn from this same chain — evidence that a standing, browsable interface on top of LOTUS-derived estimates is achievable, not just aspirational.

Model logic

The calibrated take μ is now anchored to a real observed count rather than synthesized from environmental conditions alone. The LOTUS-observed interactions, expanded by observer coverage, are the base; everything else is a multiplicative adjustment factor on top of that base, applied before the Bayesian projection:

  • Expanded estimate = observed interactions (LOTUS) ÷ observer coverage rate — a design-based expansion, shown directly in Table 0.
  • Environmental adjustment factor = (weighted SST/EKE/|vorticity|/chlorophyll-a) ÷ the same formula evaluated at baseline sensor values, so it equals 1.0× when conditions are at baseline and moves proportionally as conditions shift.
  • Spatial overlap multiplier = 1 + (southward shift ÷ 100) × 0.5
  • Gear multiplier = 1 + (shallow-set share ÷ 100) × 0.3 × (1 − 0.5 × compliance)
  • μcalibrated = expanded estimate × environmental factor × β (if bias correction on) × spatial multiplier × gear multiplier — this is the model-based figure the Dashboard stats and the ITS trigger check use.
  • Demographic penalty multiplier = 1 + max(0, juvenile share − 30%) ÷ 100 × 1.5, applied to the sigmoidal penalty, not to μ — juveniles raise population-level severity without changing the raw count.

This mirrors a real design-based vs. model-based distinction in fisheries reporting: Table 0's expanded estimate is what a straightforward coverage expansion of the observer data would give you; μcalibrated is what the risk model expects once current conditions are factored in. The gap between the two is itself informative — a large gap means conditions this cycle are unusual relative to what was actually observed.

Random forest ensembles for anomaly detection

The Dashboard's sigmoidal/Bayesian logic above is a transparent, single-threshold check on total take against the ITS. It is univariate — it doesn't ask why a year looks unusual. A random forest ensemble is proposed as the Stage 3 complement that reasons over all abstracted covariates at once, including the LOTUS-sourced bycatch and demographic fields in Table 0: spatial shift, gear shift, gear compliance, avoidance response, juvenile share, SST, EKE, vorticity, and chlorophyll-a, per species-year.

  • Ensemble structure — many decision trees, each trained on a bootstrap sample of historical species-years with a random subset of the covariates at each split; the forest's output is the average (regression) or vote share (classification) across trees, which is what makes it robust to any single noisy input or short data run.
  • Two complementary modes:
    • Supervised — if past years have been labeled "flagged / not flagged" by the Plan Team, the forest learns a classifier and outputs an anomaly probability for the current year, alongside the ITS trigger check already surfaced in Table 3 and the Dashboard alert box.
    • Unsupervised (isolation-style) — since true anomalies are rare and not always pre-labeled, trees can instead be grown to isolate unusual covariate combinations quickly (short average path length = more anomalous); this needs no labeled training set, which matters early in the pipeline's life when few confirmed anomaly-years exist.
  • Feature importance — the forest ranks which covariates most influenced a given year's anomaly score (e.g. "spatial shift and juvenile share drove 70% of this year's score, oceanographic terms drove the rest"). This ranking populates the Table 3 diagnostic rows directly and doubles as a live VOI signal — the covariate the forest weights most heavily is the one worth investing further monitoring in.
  • Confidence, not a hard cutoff — out-of-bag error and the spread of votes across trees give an honest confidence band on the anomaly call, which sits next to (not instead of) the existing exceedance-probability and utility readouts on the Dashboard.

This section documents the intended Stage 3 methodology; the interactive Dashboard above still runs the transparent formulas in Model logic so every number on screen stays auditable. Wiring an actual trained forest in would be the next build step, likely running server-side against real historical species-year records pulled from LOTUS rather than in-browser.

Anomaly detection logic

The formula-based check and the random forest check above feed a single combined decision. It is an OR-gate escalation, not a consensus vote: any one signal firing is enough to move a species-year to the next tier, because real anomalies in this fishery show up in different forms — a pure count blip, a demographic shift with a normal total, or a spatial/gear pattern that's statistically odd without exceeding any count. Requiring every check to agree would let a genuine single-signal anomaly slip through unflagged.

  • 1. Species-year inputs — the abstracted covariates already assembled in Table 0/1: spatial shift, gear shift, gear compliance, avoidance response, juvenile share, SST, EKE, vorticity, chlorophyll-a.
  • 2a. Formula-based check — two existing sub-rules, either one sufficient to flag: (i) does current-year take exceed the ITS trigger threshold (ITS limit × (1 − dynamic buffer), where the buffer widens as SST/EKE rise)? this is what populates Table 3 and its "FLAGGED FOR REVIEW" badge. (ii) does the Bayesian exceedance probability cross the risk-posture inflection point, or does utility go negative? this is what sets the Dashboard alert box.
  • 2b. Multivariate check — the random forest ensemble score from the section above, run in parallel on the same inputs; it can flag an unusual combination of covariates even when neither formula-based sub-rule is crossed.
  • 3. Combined decision — because the gate is OR, not AND: nothing crossed → open access; something trending toward a threshold without crossing it → elevated watch; anything actually crossed (ITS trigger, utility/probability, or the RF anomaly score) → review triggered.
  • 4. Outcome — review triggered is what populates Table 3 and drafts the auto-narrative; a Plan Team scientist reviews and edits before anything reaches the SAFE report, per the auto-narrative note in the Summary Tables tab.

Today only 2a is implemented in the live Dashboard; 2b (the random forest layer) is documented above as the intended next build, and the combined OR-gate is currently expressed as two separate indicators (the Table 3 "FLAGGED FOR REVIEW" badge and the Dashboard alert box) rather than one unified status — merging them into a single displayed decision would be a natural follow-on to wiring in the forest. Table 2 (Summary Tables tab) is a separate, real historical monitoring reference and does not participate in this OR-gate.

Known defensibility gap

The rolling 5-year running-sum ITS is legally workable under 50 CFR §402.16 but is not population-referenced — it reflects historical takes, not PBR or λ. Anchoring the threshold to a demographic model (life-history synthesis → stage-structured model → elasticity analysis → ITS derivation from PBR/λ → peer review → pipeline integration) is the identified path to a more defensible threshold and remains a future workstream this tool does not resolve. See "Toward a Bayesian, population-referenced ITS" below for the proposed approach.

Toward a Bayesian, population-referenced ITS

The rolling max is a descriptive statistic, not an inference — it answers "what's the highest take we've seen lately," not "what level of take the population can sustain." It has no probabilistic interpretation, it's blind to stock trend (a declining and a recovering stock with the same recent takes get the same threshold), and it discards partial information that already exists for these species (life history studies, regional stock assessments, tagging data). This section documents the proposed replacement, which is the same six-phase workstream already tracked in this app's plan, made concrete.

  • Reference point, not a lagging statistic — derive the threshold from a Potential Biological Removal–style calculation, the same logic MMPA already applies to marine mammals, extended here to ESA-listed species: PBR = Nmin × ½Rmax × Fr, where Nmin is a conservative population estimate, Rmax is maximum net productivity, and Fr is a recovery factor tuned to stock status.
  • Bayesian treatment because the data are genuinely sparse — Nmin and Rmax for OWT and turtles aren't known precisely; put a prior on each, update with whatever survey/tagging/assessment data exists, and get a posterior distribution rather than a false-precision point estimate. The ITS becomes "the take level such that posterior P(exceeding a sustainable removal rate) < an agreed tolerance" — a real probability statement, not a historical maximum.
  • SME input as formal priors, not ad hoc opinion — structured elicitation (IDEA protocol or SHELF-style), multiple independent experts, aggregated and calibrated rather than one biologist's gut number, with the elicitation protocol itself documented. That documentation is what turns "expert judgment" into "best scientific and commercial data available" in the regulatory sense, rather than something a challenger can dismiss as subjective.
  • Plugs directly into the existing Dashboard — the Bayesian Risk Projection chart already computes an exceedance probability against the ITS Limit slider. Under this approach that slider becomes a model output with a credible interval (the posterior PBR-referenced threshold) rather than a user-set constant. The random forest layer is unaffected — it still explains why a year looks unusual, while the population model says whether the magnitude matters biologically.

Staying inline with regulations: 50 CFR §402.16 requires NMFS to specify a take amount/extent and reinitiate consultation if exceeded — it does not prescribe the calculation method, so switching methodologies is permissible. Three things have to hold for it to survive scrutiny:

  • Best available science standard (ESA §7(a)(2), MSA National Standard 2) — the model and elicitation protocol need peer review, the same peer-review step already in the six-phase plan.
  • Transparency and audit trail — every prior, data source, and elicitation round documented, matching the Data Provenance card already in the Pipeline tab.
  • Formal process, not a silent swap — changing the ITS methodology means reinitiating or amending the Biological Opinion through PIRO/PIFSC; a tool can propose and simulate the new threshold, but cannot unilaterally replace the regulatory number without that consultation process.
